Volkswagen Group approved production in 2001, but development ran four
years past its original target as Bugatti and Volkswagen's engineers worked
through problems that a car with this specification had never had to solve
before: an engine that could run flat-out for hours without failing, tyres
rated for over 400 km/h, and enough cooling for a 1,000 PS-plus engine
mounted just behind the cabin.[@wikipedia-bugatti-veyron] The result set an
independently measured top speed record on Volkswagen's own Ehra-Lessien
track in April 2005, before the car had even gone on sale.

The 2010 Super Sport pushed the same basic W16 further, to a Guinness
World Records-confirmed 431.07 km/h, a record later passed to Bugatti's
own Chiron. Both trims share a single published 0-100 km/h figure in
Wikipedia's own performance table, credited jointly to the "Standard, Grand
Sport" and "Super Sport, Grand Sport Vitesse" pairs rather than broken out
per trim, which this entry records as given rather than inventing a faster
number for the more powerful car.
